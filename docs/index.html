These registers publish
the present tense. Only.

Twenty-one European public registers list who is licensed to run a bank, issue a stablecoin, sell a medical device or run a clinical trial. Not one of them offers a changelog, a ?since= parameter or a feed. The EBA overwrites its file nightly, FINMA every morning, ESMA weekly, Austria monthly. Yesterday's version is not archived anywhere. It is simply gone. Everything below was counted on one day. The column saying what moved exists only because something kept the earlier copy.

Reference

The obligations behind the rows — and which of them keep a list

Every firm counted on this page carries a stack of requirements, and they are not the same kind of thing. Some are law: they bind whether or not anyone asks, a public authority can refuse you, and refusal power implies a register — which is why this page can count them at all. Others are contract: a customer or a card scheme requires them, an auditor checks them, and nothing about the outcome is published. ISO 27001 and PCI DSS are the control group for this entire project. They are seriously enforced and completely invisible. This is a map for orientation, not legal advice.

Law · an authority can refuse you, so there is a register counted on this page where the register is public
MiFID II / MiFIR Dir. 2014/65/EU · Reg. 600/2014 3 Jan 2018
Investment firms, trading venues, systematic internalisers. Authorisation by the national competent authority, passportable across the EEA.
Public register. ESMA's upreg core — 5,319 active investment-firm head offices counted here, with each firm's authorised services. The caveat proved above still applies: which services appear depends on what the national authority chooses to report.
MiCA Reg. (EU) 2023/1114 30 Jun 2024 · 30 Dec 2024
Crypto-asset service providers, asset-referenced and e-money token issuers, white-paper notifiers. Titles III–IV applied first; the CASP regime from 30 December 2024.
Public register. ESMA's interim MiCA register — 349 authorised CASPs, 0 ART issuers, 49 EMT issuers, and under Article 110 a register of 174 firms operating without authorisation. All listed and downloadable above.
DORA Reg. (EU) 2022/2554 17 Jan 2025
Around twenty categories of financial entity — including every investment firm and every CASP on this page. ICT risk management, incident classification and reporting, resilience testing, and third-party contractual requirements.
Mostly not public. Each entity maintains a register of information on its ICT third-party arrangements and reports it to its supervisor — supervisory, not published. What is public is the other end: the ESAs designate and publish critical ICT third-party providers. So DORA tells you who the systemically important vendors are, never who uses them.
Transfer of Funds Regulation Reg. (EU) 2023/1113 · the travel rule 30 Dec 2024
Originator and beneficiary information on crypto-asset transfers. Binds CASPs and payment service providers, and arrived with MiCA deliberately.
No register of its own. Compliance rides on the MiCA authorisation — the CASP list above is the closest public proxy for who is in scope.
PSD2 Dir. (EU) 2015/2366 · register under Impl. Reg. (EU) 2019/410 13 Jan 2018
Payment institutions, e-money institutions, account information and payment initiation providers, plus their agents and branches.
Public register, and required to be machine-readable by law. The EBA's nightly golden copy — 6,427 institutions counted here. PSD3 and the Payment Services Regulation are set to replace it — check where they stand before relying on this line.
NIS2 Dir. (EU) 2022/2555 transposition due 17 Oct 2024
Essential and important entities across eighteen sectors. For financial entities DORA is lex specialis, so the firms on this page answer to DORA rather than NIS2 for ICT risk.
No single register. Member states register entities nationally, at differing speeds and with differing publication practice. There is no EU-level list to count.
GDPR Reg. (EU) 2016/679 25 May 2018
Anyone processing personal data. No authorisation step, so no approval list — which is precisely why there is nothing to enumerate.
No register of controllers. What is published is adversarial: supervisory decisions, fines and the EDPB's records. This project applies the rule to itself — the ~322,000 PSD2 agent records are largely natural persons and are excluded by default.
AML package Reg. (EU) 2024/1624 · AMLA Reg. (EU) 2024/1620 from 10 Jul 2027
Obliged entities, explicitly including crypto-asset service providers. Supervision centralises under the new authority, AMLA.
Restricted. Beneficial-ownership registers exist at member-state level, but general public access was struck down in WM and Sovim (C-37/20) in November 2022; access now requires legitimate interest and varies by country.
MDR Reg. (EU) 2017/745 26 May 2021
Medical device manufacturers, importers, authorised representatives, procedure-pack producers — and the notified bodies that certify them.
Public register. EUDAMED — 49,832 economic operators and 70 notified bodies counted here, with the registered contact details the regulation requires.
Clinical Trials Regulation Reg. (EU) 536/2014 31 Jan 2022
Sponsors of clinical trials conducted in the EU, and the trials themselves.
Public register. CTIS — 12,467 authorised trials, though its API will page through only the first 10,000 while reporting the true total.
Contract · seriously enforced, and no list anywhere the control group for this whole method
ISO/IEC 27001 :2022 edition 2013 transition closed 31 Oct 2025
An information security management system, certified by an accredited certification body. Almost always driven by a customer's procurement requirement rather than by law.
No public register of certified organisations. National accreditation bodies publish the certification bodies they accredit — not those bodies' clients. So the only way to learn that a company holds 27001 is that the company tells you, which is will, not obligation. Treat a trust-page badge accordingly: verify the certificate number and scope with the issuing body.
PCI DSS v4 series · PCI Security Standards Council v3.2.1 retired 31 Mar 2024 · future-dated v4 requirements from 31 Mar 2025
Anyone storing, processing or transmitting cardholder data. Not law: it binds through the card schemes' contracts and the acquirer relationship, with validation effort set by merchant or service-provider level.
No public register of compliant entities. The Council publishes validated assessors (QSA, ASV) and validated payment software and devices; the card schemes maintain their own service-provider registries. A merchant's own compliance status is a matter between it, its acquirer and its assessor.
SOC 2 AICPA attestation Type I / Type II reports
Service organisations, on customer demand. An auditor's attestation over the trust services criteria, covering a stated period.
No register at all. The report is delivered under non-disclosure and shared deal by deal. Nothing about it is enumerable from outside.
TIBER-EU and DORA TLPT ECB framework · DORA Arts. 26–27 TLPT under DORA from 17 Jan 2025
Threat-led penetration testing for significant financial entities, run with the supervisor's involvement and modelled on TIBER-EU.
Deliberately not public. The point of the exercise is that results stay with the entity and its authority. Which firms are even in scope is not published.

The split is the whole argument of this page. A licence is worth counting because no licensed firm can choose not to appear in the register — the authority that could refuse has to keep the list, or it cannot enforce. A certification is not countable at all, because nobody with the power to refuse publishes who they approved. Both are real obligations. Only one of them leaves a public record, and confusing the two is how a plausible market map turns out to be a map of who has a marketing page.

The twenty biggest licences

What a register can rank, and what it only appears to rank

These registers publish no revenue, no headcount and no size. The one dimension of bigness they do publish is the breadth of the authorisation itself — how many distinct services a firm is allowed to perform, and in how many countries. For the crypto register that produces a genuine ranking. For MiFID II it produces something that looks like one and is not, which is worth more than the league table would have been.

MiFID II · investment firms 5,319 active head offices · the ranking that dissolves on inspection

197 firms tie for first place, holding an identical licence

Counting distinct authorised services across every active MiFID II head office gives a maximum of sixteen, reached by 197 firms. All 197 hold the same sixteen — there is exactly one distinct service set among them, so the register offers no basis whatsoever for ordering them. A top-20 table here is a slice of a tie.

And the tie has a geography: 106 of the 197 are Spanish and 68 are Luxembourgish, with 16 Irish, 5 Dutch and 2 Romanian. Not one is German — out of 1,872 German investment firms.

That is not because German investment firms do less. It is because each national authority decides what to report to ESMA, and they do not report the same thing. Every German firm caps at exactly seven services because BaFin reports only the MiFID Annex I Section A investment services. Spain, Luxembourg, Ireland and the Netherlands also report the Section B ancillary services — advice on capital structure, foreign exchange, safekeeping, research, granting credits — which is the entire difference between seven and sixteen.

Who actually holds the crypto licence

A third of Europe's authorised crypto firms were already regulated

The MiCA register and the ESMA investment-firm register share a join key: the LEI. Matching all 349 authorised crypto-asset service providers against every MiFID II investment firm gives a direct answer to who is actually taking these licences — incumbents extending into crypto, or crypto businesses entering regulation. It is both, and the split is geographic.

128of 349 crypto firms also hold a MiFID II investment-firm authorisation, matched on LEI. All 128 are active.
81 of 91German crypto licences are held by an existing investment firm — 89%. In Malta it is 1 of 22, in Czechia 0 of 12.
19.1 vs 8.3countries passported into, on average, by crypto-native firms versus dual-licensed ones. The newcomers are the pan-European ones.
4 of 128dual-licensed firms may operate a trading platform. Exchanges are overwhelmingly crypto-native, not incumbent.

The two groups are not doing the same thing. Dual-licensed firms take the permissions that extend what they already do: execution of orders (83 of 128) and reception and transmission (45 of 128) — brokerage, with crypto as one more instrument. Crypto-native firms take the permissions a wallet and an exchange need: transfer services (177 of 221), custody (174 of 221) and exchange for funds (161 of 221). Custody is held by 79% of the crypto natives and 38% of the incumbents.

Read the German rows and the 89% stops being mysterious. Of the 91 German crypto licences, 43 are held by cooperative and regional banks — Volksbanken, Raiffeisenbanken, VR-Banken — and 42 of those hold exactly one permission, execution of orders, in exactly one country. Seventy-four of the 91 are single-country. Seventeen hold custody; two may operate a trading platform. The average German licence covers 1.86 services and 5.2 countries, against Malta's 24.0 countries. Germany did not license 91 crypto businesses. It let its retail banks pass a customer's crypto order through, which is a different event that produces the same row in a register — and is exactly the kind of thing a count alone would get wrong.

Four further firms match a MiFID II investment firm by normalised name only, because the LEI in the crypto register does not appear in the investment-firm register. Those are marked separately below and should not be treated as the same fact: a matching name is evidence, an identifier is proof. Four more appear in the ESMA register as fund managers rather than investment firms. The remaining 213 hold no ESMA authorisation at all.

Why the change column is the hard part

Every one of these publishers destroys its own history

The registers are obliged to publish who is licensed. Nothing obliges them to publish who stopped being licensed, and none of them do. A licence that lapses does not appear in a "removals" feed — the row simply is not in tomorrow's file.

Nightly

The EBA's PSD2 golden copy is regenerated every night and published with its own SHA-256. The checksum proves the file is intact; it says nothing about what the file used to contain.

EBA · 6,427 institutions

Every morning

FINMA regenerates its authorisation CSV daily at a fixed URL. Fetch it at noon and you have today's list. Fetch it tomorrow and yesterday's is unrecoverable.

FINMA · 2,941 authorisations

Weekly, in place

ESMA's five MiCA CSVs are overwritten at the same five URLs. There are no dated versions and no archive, so Last-Modified is the only vintage information that exists.

MiCA · 1,573 records across five files

Monthly, as a new file

Austria publishes a fresh GISA extract each month. The previous month's file is replaced rather than kept, so a year of Austrian trade-licence history exists only where someone saved it.

GISA · 1,032,282 licences

Read the interface, not the documentation

Six ways a public register returns a confident wrong answer

None of these produce an error. Every one returns HTTP 200 with a well-formed body, and every one was found by probing the live service rather than by reading its help page.

The silent ceiling

The Swiss UID register's SOAP search returns at most 30 results and does not say so. Ask for every firm in Zürich and you get thirty of them, formatted exactly like a complete answer.

BFS · Switzerland

The honest total, the truncated list

CTIS reports totalRecords: 12,467 and then refuses to page past record 10,000. The count is right; the data behind it stops early. This client warns when the window truncates the answer.

CTIS · clinical trials

The empty search that is really a missing key

Omit searchCriteria from a CTIS request and the API answers 200 with totalRecords: 0 — a perfectly well-formed way of saying "your query was wrong". Verified again today.

CTIS · re-checked 23 Sep 2026

The /csv that is not a CSV

Austria's open-data /csv path serves a 7-Zip archive. A standard CSV reader sees binary and either crashes or, worse, parses the first few bytes into a row.

GISA · data.gv.at

The language flag that is a filter

EUDAMED's mandatory languageIso2Code looks like a display preference. Leave it out and the result set changes shape, so it silently decides which records you can see.

EUDAMED · medical devices

Parents and children in one flat array

ESMA's upreg core returns entity records and their child records in the same docs array, told apart only by a marker field. Count the array and you overstate the number of firms by a factor of seven.

ESMA · 101,771 documents → 13,913 entities

DACH

Austria and Switzerland publish files. Germany publishes a portal.

This is the opposite of what most people expect, and it decides how a German question has to be answered.

Austria — open data, no negotiation

The entire trade-licence register, 1,032,282 rows, published under CC BY 4.0 by the ministry — even though its own search page is an ASP.NET form that cannot be automated at all.

The financial regulator, by contrast, offers no interface whatsoever.

Switzerland — an unauthenticated web service

The federal business register is exposed over public SOAP with a published WSDL, and FINMA's authorisation list is a daily CSV. Switzerland is outside the EEA, so no EU register covers it.

The commercial register's REST API needs a key, and its host bans crawlers outright. It is documented here and deliberately not used.

Germany — an export button behind a robots ban

BaFin's databases do offer CSV, XML and Excel exports, and the whole portal host is robots.txt: Disallow: /. So this project does not touch it, and shows you the export URLs to click yourself.

Automated German coverage is routed through the EU-level registers instead — ESMA and the EBA both carry BaFin-supervised entities.

Where the next source comes from

Nine kinds of obligation

The list above is not a collection of favourites; it is one pass over a structure. Any economic activity leaves a trail of compulsory filings before it leaves a voluntary one, and they sort into nine kinds. The question to ask is not where is there information but who is compelled by law to tell the truth.

A
Existence and ownership
Commercial registers, LEI, beneficial ownership. gleif, and through it the German Handelsregister number.
B
Operating licences
The industry directories. upreg eba-psd mica-* finma gisa eudamed ctis
C
Technology and intellectual property
Patents, trademarks, standards bodies. Not implemented — the EPO and EUIPO interfaces both need a free key.
D
Public money
Procurement, grants, subsidies, state aid. ted. CORDIS and the German Förderkatalog are not implemented.
E
Physical facilities
Emission permits, planning consent, environmental approval. Not implemented; German BImSchG notices sit at Länder level.
F
Money and tax
Filings, prospectuses, VAT. vies. The Bundesanzeiger's annual accounts are not machine-readable.
G
Adversarial procedures
Courts, merger review, insolvency, recalls. Not implemented — and EU merger decisions are the richest free market analysis there is.
H
People
Visa sponsor lists, professional registers, association members. Not implemented; the Dutch recognised-sponsor list is the cleanest example.
I
Cross-border
FDI screening, export control, trade data. Not implemented.

Two honest limits

What five million mandatory records cannot tell you

Mandatory is not complete

These sources are blind to the informal economy, to what happens inside a company, to intent — and, most usefully to remember, to approved but abandoned. An authorisation says somebody meant to do something. It does not say they did it. A licence is not a business signal: nothing here reports headcount, revenue or whether anyone works there, and the licensed entity is frequently not the operating one. Of the 13,913 entities in the ESMA register today, 2,220 are marked inactive and 1,279 records are branches of a firm licensed somewhere else. Cross-check against a second layer — accounts, contract awards, imports — before believing a plan happened.

A list is not an outcome

Building one of these has visible progress, verifiable results and no social cost, which makes it the finest form of procrastination available. The only value of a list is the order it puts things in. After it is sorted, you still have to go and talk to people.

Check it yourself

Every number here is one command away

Nothing on this page came from a scrape. Each figure is the register's own count, and the tool that produced it is open source, has one runtime dependency, and asks no register for a key. 

# the ledger above, live — 21 registers, shape-checked
uvx --from git+https://github.com/7feilee/eufinreg eufinreg doctor

# every EU crypto-asset service provider, with the services they are licensed for
uv run eufinreg --source mica-casp -o casps.csv

# every active Austrian trade licence in Vienna
uv run eufinreg --source gisa --query 'nuts2=AT13' -o wien.csv

# keep the present tense, so next month there is a past tense to compare against
uv run eufinreg ingest --config eufinreg.config.json
uv run eufinreg watch --coverage

eufinreg reads public registers machine-to-machine. No HTML scraping happens anywhere in the project. Personal-data selections are gated in code rather than in a warning, and three perfectly usable endpoints are documented and deliberately not wrapped.

Counts taken 23 September 2026 against a baseline of 12–16 August 2026, and they will drift — that is the point. The interface shapes should not. Each register's own disclaimer travels with its rows: ESMA states that the crypto-asset white papers in its register have not been reviewed or approved by any competent authority, and the offeror is solely responsible for their content.
